A lightweight decision simulation about false closure, evidence discipline, validation gaps, and the handoffs that look complete before they are actually safe.

What this is

Broken Handoff is a support-wargaming scenario built to help teams practice the moment before a bad handoff becomes official.

The player reviews evidence, makes decisions under pressure, locks choices, and receives an after-action review showing what was preserved, ignored, transferred, or broken.

What this is not

  • Not an arcade pitch.
  • Not entertainment-first gamification.
  • Not AI deciding truth.
  • Not a predictive analytics product.
  • Not a replacement for SME judgment.

How the run works

Each run follows the same decision rhythm. The subject changes; the evidence discipline does not.

ObserveRead the reported status, friction, and initial handoff claim.
ReviewCompare evidence, gaps, pressure, AI output, and validation records.
ChooseSelect the response a real team might make under pressure.
LockCommit the decision, or reconsider before locking it.
ValidateTest whether the apparent fix survives the actual failure condition.
AARReview what the run preserved, ignored, transferred, or poisoned.

Doctrine layer

The simulation is not asking players to guess a color-coded right answer. It is testing whether the handoff survives evidence, pressure, and validation.

Status is not proof

A clean dashboard, closed ticket, completed CAP, matching count, or successful log does not prove the underlying condition is fixed.

AI is not the judge

AI output can summarize, compare, or flag inconsistencies. It does not replace source evidence or SME judgment.

Risk transfer must be explicit

Escalating or handing off risk is not automatically wrong. Silently passing unresolved risk without facts, gap, owner, and recommendation can poison the handoff.

Validation controls closure

Repair, reconciliation, closeout, or migration is not real until it works against the actual failure condition.

What the AAR shows

The output is not just a score. It is the receipt for the judgment path.

Evidence discipline

What evidence was used, what was ignored, and what appeared true before validation.

Risk posture

Where the player preserved risk, transferred risk, or accepted an unresolved gap.

Handoff quality

Whether the next team inherited facts, owner, recommendation, and validation requirement — or a poisoned handoff.

Where this could go

The near-term claim is decision rehearsal. The future-state question is whether repeated scenario runs create enough context to support useful pattern analysis.

The working hypothesis

We suspect support wargaming could help teams prepare for predictive analysis by creating better context around how decisions actually fail.

The simulation does not predict audit failure, supply failure, ticket failure, or migration failure today. It captures structured decision data: what looked complete, what evidence was trusted, where pressure entered, what validation was skipped, and what risk was accepted.

If repeated runs produce consistent patterns, those patterns may later inform risk indicators, advisory support, training priorities, workflow redesign, and eventually predictive analysis. That future state has to be earned by evidence, not assumed from the prototype.

What better context means

Most dashboards show status. Broken Handoff is designed to capture the judgment path behind the status.

  • What evidence was available?
  • What did the team trust too early?
  • What warning flag was recognized but not preserved?
  • Where did ownership become unclear?
  • What risk was fixed, deferred, transferred, or accepted?
  • What would the next team need to know?

Data maturity path

This is intentionally conservative. The method should not claim prediction before the data proves it can support analysis.

1

Playable scenario

Run one support problem through the evidence, pressure, decision, validation, and AAR loop.

2

Structured AAR

Capture the decision path, evidence used, evidence ignored, ownership gap, and validation failure.

3

Repeated runs

Run similar scenarios across teams, roles, or functions to see whether the same failure patterns repeat.

4

Pattern analysis

Compare runs for recurring risk-transfer behaviors, false closure points, evidence gaps, and rework loops.

5

Risk indicators

Use repeated patterns to identify warning flags that may deserve earlier review, training, or control attention.

6

Future analytics

Explore predictive support only after the scenario data is reliable, repeatable, and tied to real outcomes.

Near-term output

Better conversations, clearer handoffs, sharper AARs, and stronger evidence discipline.

Future-state output

Possible pattern libraries, risk indicators, control-gap themes, and data-informed advisory support.

What we will not claim yet

That the prototype predicts failures, replaces SMEs, or proves a statistical model.

What would make it credible

Repeated runs, consistent structured capture, SME review, and comparison against real-world outcomes.

Before you play

Do not play to guess the interface. Make the call you think a real support team would make under pressure.

Some decisions are doctrine-aligned. Some are incomplete but recoverable. Some transfer risk. Some poison the handoff by accepting status, AI confidence, or paperwork as proof when validation is still missing.

Play like the next team, auditor, user, or commander has to live with your handoff.
