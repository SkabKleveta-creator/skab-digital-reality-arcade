01 · Know the family

“F/A-18” is a family name, not one airplane.

The original Hornet is the A–D series. The Navy’s carrier-air-wing workhorse is the larger F/A-18E/F Super Hornet. E is single-seat; F is two-seat.

Why it matters to an AE

Platform names hide major differences. “F/A-18” alone does not tell you which configuration, age, or systems baseline you are discussing. Start every conversation by getting the exact model and configuration.

02 · Power is a system

Think path, not part.

An electrical function is never just a box. At the broadest level, it depends on generation, control, conversion, distribution, protection, the load, and a way to show the crew what is happening.

The big AE habit

When a function fails, disciplined maintainers ask what part of the system is actually being observed—not which component they hope is bad. The authorized publication determines the actual action. Maintainer translation: “it only does it sometimes” is a description, not a diagnosis.

03 · Block III changes the question

More capability means more integration.

Public Navy reporting describes Block III upgrades as adding a modernized cockpit, enhanced networking, avionics upgrades, and electrical modernization to support them.

Plain-English translation

The aircraft is not frozen in time. When computing, displays, data, and sensors change, they change the supporting system story too. Integration is a readiness issue, not just an engineering word.

04 · Two engines, one mission

The Super Hornet is a carrier-suitable twin-engine strike fighter.

Its job is deliberately broad: fighter escort, fleet air defense, strike, reconnaissance, close air support, and aerial refueling are among its publicly described mission sets.

Geek fact

Public NAVAIR specifications list two F414-GE-400 turbofan engines, each rated at 22,000 pounds of static thrust. The point is not the number—it is that electrical capability supports a jet built to do more than one job.

05 · Carrier reality

Naval aviation is a team sport in a brutal environment.

Salt air, vibration, heat cycles, time pressure, limited space, corrosion risk, and shift handoffs all shape readiness. A clean handoff and accurate documentation protect the next maintainer and the crew.

What “professional” looks like

Knowing the system matters. Knowing where the authorized process starts, what was observed, and how to communicate it clearly matters just as much. That is how individual work becomes safe aircraft availability. A good handoff is a kindness to the next maintainer—and a strong defense against a mystery growing legs overnight.

06 · AE has a wide lane

Electricity touches almost every aircraft story.

The Navy’s AE occupational standard publicly includes power generation, conversion and distribution; batteries; lighting; and electrical control of systems including landing gear, flight controls, warnings, and more.

Important distinction

That does not mean one person “owns the jet.” It means AEs must see how their work connects to airframe, propulsion, instruments, avionics, quality assurance, and maintenance control.

Field note · built by a system

A jet in the sky is the visible part.

Underneath it: people, timing, tools, data, and the person willing to say “hold up.” Less cinematic. Much more impressive.

The jokes are optional. The standards, authorized data, and the people beside you are not.

The funniest parts of maintenance are usually the same things that teach good habits: no invisible gremlins, no magical shortcuts, and no such thing as “probably good enough” when a crew will trust the work.

“It worked yesterday.”

Yesterday is a witness, not a maintenance record.
