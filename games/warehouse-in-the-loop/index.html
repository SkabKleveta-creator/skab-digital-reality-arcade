Warehouse-in-the-Loop

A facilitated warehouse decision game about proof, pressure, and human processing.

Warehouse-in-the-Loop is not here to embarrass the floor, blame Supply Marines, or pretend a browser page is a warehouse management system. It is a safe-to-fail support wargame that helps a team see where records, handoffs, priorities, and human comprehension can drift apart before the real mission pays for it.

So What

The point is not warehouse trivia. The point is decision friction.

Warehouses usually do not fail because one person forgot how to work. They fail when the floor, the record, the priority, and the handoff stop telling the same story. WITL turns that failure mode into a short, playable diagnostic.

What it reveals

Where process, data, people, and priority disagree before the team commits to the wrong action.

What it trains

A team slows just enough to understand the next action, then acts with evidence instead of noise.

What it proves

Validated flow beats false throughput. A documented hold can be better than a dirty ship.

Doctrine

Humans need time and context before the next right action.

The core lesson

The Processing Pause is not delay for delay's sake. It is the moment where the team names what it knows, what it is assuming, what could hurt people or evidence, and what would prove the action worked.

The respect rule

This is not a blame tool. If the team is overloaded, confused, or working from bad records, the game treats that as a system condition to diagnose, not a character flaw.

Language guardrail: do not frame the run as “catching mistakes.” Frame it as finding where the work became harder, less clear, or less provable than it needed to be.

Run Loop

One decision moment at a time.

1. ReadbackWhat changed?
2. PauseWhat do we need to understand?
3. CommitX because Y; worked when Z.
4. ActionObserve, Stabilize, Route, Exception, Validate.
5. ResolveProvisional, validated, or risk.
6. RecordWhat changed and what remains unknown?
7. DecisionShip, hold, or accept false-win risk.
8. AARWhat did the run teach?

Digital Console

The digital layer supports the table. It does not replace judgment.

Timing and rhythm

The console guides the readback, pause, commit, action, resolution, final decision, and AAR so the facilitator does not have to hold everything in memory.

Controlled reveals

Ground truth stays facilitator-controlled. The console does not invent facts or tell players the best move.

Evidence capture

The console separates provisional gains from validated gains and records what the team believed, proved, or left open.

Activity Visualization

The warehouse board becomes a decision surface.

The next digital direction is not a full simulator. It is an event-history layer: every focus, action, reveal, validation, and risk marker becomes a structured event that can drive the board and the AAR.

What the board can show

  • Where the team spent attention
  • Which zones accumulated risk
  • What was provisional vs. validated
  • Where hidden faults were revealed or proven

Why that matters

Across repeated runs, the structured event history can show decision patterns, validation gaps, handoff failures, and recovery behavior. That is a data foundation for pattern analysis later, not a predictive claim today.

Guardrails

What this should not become yet.

Not a WMS

No live inventory, no official records, no unit data, no operational authority.

Not a load planner

No cube, weight, tie-down, embark, or transportation calculations in this lane.

Not AI command advice

AI may help structure memory, logs, and AARs. It should not recommend moves, generate facts, or replace the facilitator.

Current proof question: Can a short facilitated run help a team see where warehouse work became unclear, unproven, or overloaded — without making the people doing the work feel blamed?
